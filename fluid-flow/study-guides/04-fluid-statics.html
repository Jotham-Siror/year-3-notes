2 · Absolute vs gauge pressure

Two reference points
Measured from a perfect vacuum ⇒ absolute pressure. Measured from local atmospheric pressure ⇒ gauge pressure.
A gauge pressure below atmospheric is negative (partial vacuum). Most engineering pressures are quoted as gauge unless "absolute" is stated.

3 · The laws of pressure & the hydraulic press

Three characteristics
1. Fluid pressure always acts perpendicular to the surface in contact.
2. At any point in a fluid at rest, pressure is the same in all directions (isotropic).
3. Pascal's law: pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part.

The hydraulic press is Pascal's law at work: the same pressure acts on a small and a large piston, so a small input force produces a large output force.

7 · Pressure gauges (Bourdon & others)

Elastic gauge
Balances the fluid pressure against the deformation of an elastic solid.

The Bourdon tube (Eugène Bourdon, 1849) is the classic example: a curved metal tube of elliptical cross-section, sealed and free at one end, fixed at the other. Pressure entering the tube makes the cross-section rounder, which straightens the tube slightly; the free end's (amplified) motion drives the needle. Simple, accurate, and good to very high pressures.

Electric gauges convert that deformation (via a diaphragm, bellows or Bourdon tube) into an electrical signal using a wire or semiconductor strain gauge (piezoresistance) — strain gauges are especially useful for fluctuating pressures.
