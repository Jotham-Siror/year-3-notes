1 · Matter & the continuum

Big idea. A fluid is anything that keeps deforming under a shear force — it can't resist shear the way a solid can. Two properties define how a fluid behaves: compressibility and viscosity.
Solid vs fluid
A solid resists tension, compression and shear elastically (it springs back). A fluid can't sustain a shear stress at rest — apply one and it flows continuously.
The two defining properties
Compressibility — a fluid builds up pressure to resist a reduction in volume.
Viscosity — a fluid resists two layers sliding over each other.

As a rule of thumb: liquids are treated as incompressible (unless very highly pressurised — e.g. oil in a hydraulic ram), while gases are compressible (unless the pressure change is small, when compressibility can be ignored).

The continuum assumption
Molecules are in constant random motion, but the molecular mean free path is tiny compared with any flow dimension. So we ignore the molecules and treat the fluid as a continuous, isotropic substance — properties like ρ, p, v are defined at every point.
⚠ Watch out — slide 33. The slide gives the mean free path as "about 0.06 pm". That's — smaller than an atom, which is impossible. Air's molecular mean free path at STP is ≈ 68 nm ≈ 0.068 µm, so the intended unit is µm, not pm. If a value like this ever appears in a question, use ~0.07 µm.
Ideal / perfect fluid vs perfect gas
A perfect (ideal) gas obeys Boyle's–Charles' law. A fluid with no viscosity and no compressibility does not exist; a fluid with compressibility but no viscosity is called a perfect fluid (a useful idealisation for the Bernoulli analysis in Topic 06).

Kinematic viscosity (m²/s) — viscosity "per unit density", the form that appears in the Reynolds number.

Temperature dependence (know the direction!)
Gases: μ increases with T (hotter ⇒ more molecular motion & momentum exchange).
Liquids: μ decreases with T (hotter ⇒ molecules separate, cohesion falls).

6 · Surface tension

Big idea. A liquid surface behaves like a stretched elastic film: it pulls itself to the smallest possible area, which is why small drops are spherical.
