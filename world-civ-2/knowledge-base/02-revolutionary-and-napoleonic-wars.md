# 02 — The French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, 1792-1815

**How this file is built.** Two source documents cover this ground and overlap by roughly 60%. Neither
records where it came from, so nothing here is attributed to a named work, and neither one's wording is
reproduced — this is a reorganisation of what they say, with every claim cited to its block. Where they
**agree**, treat them as one witness, not two: both appear to descend from the same standard account. Where
they **disagree**, this file says so instead of choosing.

**They also scope the subject differently**, and that difference is worth an essay paragraph on its own.
`RNW` runs the wars from **1792**, making them one continuous conflict that begins as a revolutionary war and
turns into a war of conquest ·RNW ¶2. `NW` starts at **1799** and treats the Napoleonic Wars as their own
thing ·NW ¶1. **[added]** The first framing makes Napoleon the Revolution's heir; the second makes him its
replacement — neither source says so, but the choice between them shapes every judgement in §2.6.

- [added] `01` ends where this file's story begins. The last thing the handout says is that war "inaugurated
  the reign of terror" ·FR p9 — and then your notes stop. The Terror, Thermidor and the Directory are the
  gap between the two files, and neither source covers them either. See the gap map in `00-index.md`.

## 2.1 The wars in outline ·RNW ¶2

- [def] The **French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars** were a series of wars **between 1792 and 1815**,
  setting France against **shifting alliances of other European powers**, and producing a brief **French
  hegemony over most of Europe**. ·RNW ¶2
- The division the sources draw:
  - The **revolutionary wars**, conveniently concluded by **1801**, were fought first to **defend** and then
    to **spread the effects of the French Revolution**. ·RNW ¶2
  - With Napoleon's rise to absolute power, France's war aims **reverted to simple aggrandizement of influence
    and territory**. ·RNW ¶2
- **Seven coalitions** opposed France across the 23 years from 1793. ·RNW ¶4
- [added] That one sentence about war aims — defence, then export, then aggrandizement — is the spine of any
  question asking whether Napoleon continued or betrayed the Revolution. Both sources supply evidence for
  each side of it; §2.6 collects it.

### The scale of the armies ·RNW ¶4

| Period | Typical army |
|---|---|
| mid-eighteenth-century Continental battles | 60,000-70,000 troops |
| after 1800, Napoleon's routine manoeuvres | 250,000 |
| the Russian invasion of 1812 | some 600,000 |

- The mechanism behind the jump was the **levée en masse** — the revolutionary government's decree placing
  **all Frenchmen at the disposal of the army**, which raised armies of unprecedented size. ·RNW ¶4
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·RNW ¶4 — printed "levy en masse"; the term is **levée en masse**. Cosmetic; **C21**.
- [added] This is the single most important military fact in the period, and it connects straight back to
  `01`. The Revolution abolished the distinction between the nation and the army; a state that can conscript
  its whole population can lose battles and still win wars. It is also why the coalitions kept re-forming:
  no single defeat was decisive against France.

## 2.2 Monarchies at war with the French Republic, 1792-1795 ·RNW ¶4

- The **overthrow of Louis XVI and the establishment of republican government** placed France at odds with the
  monarchical and dynastic governments of the rest of Europe. ·RNW ¶4
- **Declaration of Pillnitz (1791)** — Austria and Prussia issued a provocative general call to European
  rulers to help the French king re-establish himself in power. ·RNW ¶4
- **France declared war in April 1792.** ·RNW ¶4
  > [added] **Cross-check.** `01` says France declared war "against Prussia and Austria" ·FR p9, which is
  > wrong — see **V1**. This source says only "France declared war", without naming the target, so it does
  > not repeat the error. The correct fact: **war was declared on Austria alone, 20 April 1792**, and Prussia
  > entered afterwards as Austria's ally.
- **20 September 1792 — Valmy.** French forces under **Dumouriez** and **Kellermann** turned back an invading
  **Prussian-Austrian force**. By **November** the French had **occupied all of Belgium**. ·RNW ¶4
- **Early 1793** — **Austria, Prussia, Spain, the United Provinces and Great Britain** formed the **First
  Coalition**, the first of seven that would oppose France over the next 23 years. ·RNW ¶4
- **By early 1795** France had **defeated the allies on every front**, pushing to **Amsterdam, the Rhine and
  the Pyrenees**. ·RNW ¶6
- More importantly, **Prussia was forced out of the coalition** and signed a **separate peace that held until
  1806**. ·RNW ¶6
- **May 1795** — the United Provinces of the Netherlands became the French-influenced **Batavian Republic**.
  ·RNW ¶6
- [added] Valmy is a small battle with an outsized place in the syllabus: a conscript revolutionary army
  stopped a professional dynastic one, and the Republic was proclaimed the next day. If you need one moment
  where the Revolution proved it could defend itself, this is it.

## 2.3 The rise of Napoleon, 1796-1807

### Italy and Egypt ·RNW ¶6-8

- In northern Italy a well-positioned French army threatened Austrian-Sardinian forces, but its commander was
  reluctant to move. **In March 1796 he was replaced by a more dynamic general, Napoleon Bonaparte.** ·RNW ¶6
- Napoleon's **campaign of manoeuvre** against Austrian and Sardinian forces in Italy produced the **Treaty of
  Campo Formio**, which forced Austria to: ·RNW ¶7
  - **cede the Austrian Netherlands** (now Belgium and Luxembourg) — the **first territorial additions to the
    French Republic**; and
  - **recognize the Cisalpine and Ligurian republics** established by French power in northern Italy.
- **Egypt was a major failure.** Napoleon sailed an army there in **May 1798** intending to conquer the
  Ottoman Empire. **Nelson destroyed a French naval squadron at the Battle of the Nile (1 August 1798)**,
  leaving him without naval support; after **failing to take Acre in 1799** he withdrew to France. His army
  **occupied Egypt until 1801**. ·RNW ¶8
- Meanwhile French forces established republican regimes in **Rome**, **Switzerland (the Helvetic Republic)**
  and **Italian Piedmont**. ·RNW ¶8
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·RNW ¶8 — the source names the Piedmontese regime "the Parthenopean". The **Parthenopean
  > Republic was at Naples** (January-June 1799), not in Piedmont; the Piedmontese client state was the
  > **Subalpine Republic**. Substantive — it puts a real republic in the wrong half of Italy; **V6**.
- The **Second Coalition** formed in response: **Britain, Russia, the Ottoman Empire, Naples, Portugal and
  Austria**. Its early successes were reversed by the allies' **inability to agree on strategy**. ·RNW ¶8

### Brumaire and the Consulate ·RNW ¶8, ·NW ¶2

- **9 November 1799** — the **coup d'état of 18 Brumaire, year VIII**, made Napoleon **First Consul**. By
  then, the danger of foreign intervention against the Revolution was over. ·RNW ¶8
- The second source puts it as an overthrow of the **Directory**, with Napoleon coming to power as leader of
  the **Consulate**, after the Revolution had quieted into the **Thermidorean Reaction**. ·NW ¶2
- Napoleon consolidated his rule by: ·NW ¶2
  - **suppressing rebellions** in France;
  - **normalizing relations with the Church** in the **Concordat of 1801**;
  - **streamlining French law** in the **Napoleonic Code**.
- **By 1804 he was powerful enough to declare himself Emperor.** ·NW ¶2
- [added] The Consulate is where the two sources' framings visibly diverge, and it is the best place to see
  it. `RNW` treats Brumaire as the end of the Revolution's danger; `NW` treats it as the end of the
  Revolution. Both are defensible; say which you are using.
- [added] Neither source explains the **Directory** it says Napoleon overthrew, and neither does your handout.
  That is the largest single gap in this knowledge base.

### Victory over the coalitions ·RNW ¶8, ·NW ¶3

- **1800 — Marengo.** Victory over Austria, and the consequent **Treaty of Lunéville**, left France **the
  dominant power on the Continent**. ·RNW ¶8
- For two years afterwards **only Great Britain, with its powerful navy, opposed Napoleon**. ·RNW ¶8
- **21 October 1805 — Trafalgar.** Nelson's victory **ended the French threat to invade England**. ·RNW ¶8
  The second source calls it **just about the only blemish** on Napoleon's record in the first decade of the
  century. ·NW ¶3 The two readings measure different things — strategic effect against reputation — and are
  complementary rather than conflicting; recorded as **D3**.
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·RNW ¶8 — the source narrates Trafalgar (October 1805) and *then* says "In 1805 a Third
  > Coalition formed", inverting the order: the **Third Coalition was formed in the spring/summer of 1805**,
  > before Trafalgar, and Ulm was fought days before it. Reads as a compression rather than a claim, but the
  > sequence as printed is wrong. Cosmetic; **C22**.
- **1805 — Third Coalition**: Britain, Russia and Austria. ·RNW ¶8
- Napoleon's victories, as the two sources list them: ·RNW ¶8, ·NW ¶3

| Battle | Year | Defeated |
|---|---|---|
| Marengo | 1800 | Austria |
| Ulm | 1805 | Austria |
| Austerlitz | 1805 | Austria and Russia |
| Jena, Auerstädt, Lübeck | 1806 | Prussia (new coalition member) |
| Friedland | 1807 | Russia |

- **Treaty of Tilsit (1807)** — **Prussia halved at the Elbe** and stripped of part of Poland; the second
  source adds that it **forced Czar Alexander I to ally with Napoleon**. ·RNW ¶8, ·NW ¶3
- **Treaty of Schönbrunn (1809)**, after a brief Austrian uprising. ·RNW ¶8
- The result: **all of Europe from the English Channel to the Russian border** — except **Portugal, Sweden,
  Sardinia and Sicily** — was either part of the French Empire, under French control, or allied to France by
  treaty. ·RNW ¶8
- [added] Memorise the four exceptions. "Everywhere except Portugal, Sweden, Sardinia and Sicily" is a
  precise, quotable measure of the Empire's extent, and three of the four are islands or peninsulas that the
  Royal Navy could reach — which is the argument of §2.4 in one observation.

## 2.4 The Continental System and the Peninsular War ·RNW ¶9, ·NW ¶4

- [def] **The Berlin Decree (1806)** — an attempt to use French control of continental ports to **blockade
  Britain indirectly**. Ships that called at British ports before passing to French-controlled ports became
  **liable to seizure**. This policy is the **Continental System**. ·RNW ¶9, ·NW ¶4
- **It was not successful**, and both sources say so bluntly: instead of hurting Britain, it hurt Napoleon.
  ·RNW ¶9, ·NW ¶4

**Why it failed** — four consequences, and they are the examinable content of this section:

1. **General inhibition of European trade.** Britain responded in kind, detaining ships bound for French
   ports. ·RNW ¶9
2. **Perceived favouritism.** The French government granted licences to French merchants to trade with
   Britain anyway, which **cost Napoleon considerable political support**. ·RNW ¶9
3. **Britain emerged richer.** Though pressed at home, the British **expanded their colonial markets** and
   came out of the trade war **more prosperous than before**. ·RNW ¶9
4. **It generated two revolts.** ·NW ¶4
   - In the German lands, resentment of Napoleonic rule **started German nationalism**, and Germans moved
     towards **Romanticism as an intellectual rebellion against French Enlightenment ideas**.
   - In Spain, enforcing the System led to the **Peninsular War** — a **protracted guerrilla war that
     diverted French forces from the rest of Europe**.
- [added] The Peninsular War is the hinge between §2.4 and §2.5: it is both a *consequence* of the
  Continental System and the *proving ground* for the strategy that beat Napoleon. Note also that the Spanish
  guerrillas were fighting to **restore a Bourbon** ·NW ¶11 — the Revolution's enemy — while the Germans
  wanted more self-rule. The same empire provoked a conservative revolt and a liberal one at once.

## 2.5 The defeat of Napoleon, 1812-1815

### The strategy that beat him ·RNW ¶11

**Napoleon's method:**

- Move armies **rapidly** and strike **quickly, often by surprise**.
- **Prevent the coordination** of the forces opposing him, then **defeat them piecemeal**.
- This required **thorough knowledge of the terrain**, because rapid movement made proper supply impossible
  without **large-scale requisitioning in the area of operations**.

**The answer his enemies found:**

- **Maintain a threat while avoiding engagement** until coordination could be achieved.
- **Rely on strong lines of supply**, and wait — while Napoleon's troops, chasing them, **suffered from
  overextension of their own supply lines**.

**Where it was used:**

- First in the **Peninsular Campaign** by the **Duke of Wellington**, who opened up Spain using **supply lines
  through Portugal**. ·RNW ¶11
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·RNW ¶11 — the source dates this "the Peninsular Campaign of **1811**". The Peninsular War began
  > in **1808**, and Wellington's supply-and-attrition strategy was established with the **Lines of Torres
  > Vedras in 1810**. As a date for the strategy's first use, 1811 is late. Cosmetic; **C23**.
- Most dramatically by the Russian generals **Barclay de Tolly** and **Bagration** against the invasion of
  **1812** — they **simply withdrew along parallel lines**. ·RNW ¶11

### Russia, 1812 ·RNW ¶11, ·NW ¶5

- **Trigger:** at the end of **1810, Alexander I withdrew Russia from the Continental System**. ·NW ¶5
- Napoleon invaded in **1812** with some **600,000 men**. ·RNW ¶4, ·NW ¶5
- **7 September — Borodino**, the **only full-scale engagement of the campaign**. Napoleon could not win a
  decisive victory there and was **eventually forced to retreat**. ·RNW ¶11
- The Russians turned to **pursuit**. Napoleon had to march back **along the same route he had come**, now
  **stripped of forage**, through a winter in which temperatures reached **-30 °F (-35 °C)**. ·RNW ¶11
- **Losses: 500,000 men**, plus **the faith of his allies and the awe of his enemies**. ·RNW ¶11
- [added] The two sources explain the disaster differently and you should notice it. `RNW` credits **Russian
  strategy** — the parallel withdrawal that stretched his supply lines. `NW` credits **the winter** ·NW ¶5.
  The first is an argument, the second is weather. Prefer the first, and use the second as the popular
  version you are correcting.

### Collapse, 1813-1815 ·RNW ¶12, ·NW ¶5-6

- **1813** — a new coalition mustered armies that **at last outnumbered those of France**; Napoleon's allies
  **fell away one by one**. ·RNW ¶12
- **October 1813 — Leipzig.** Defeat by an international coalition of armies. ·NW ¶5
- By **late 1813** Napoleon had been **forced to withdraw west of the Rhine**. ·RNW ¶12
- **Early 1814** — invasion of France; **Paris reached in March**; **Napoleon abdicated on 6 April**. ·RNW ¶12
- **Exile to Elba**, which **lasted less than a year**. **Louis XVIII took the throne**, returning a Bourbon
  to the throne lost by Louis XVI. ·RNW ¶12, ·NW ¶6
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·NW ¶6 — the source says the throne had been "lost by Louis XVI just **twenty years** earlier".
  > Louis XVI was deposed in **August-September 1792**, which is **22 years** before 1814. Loose
  > approximation rather than an error, but do not quote the figure. Cosmetic; **C24**.
- **March 1815** — Napoleon **returned to France and raised a new army**: the period known as the **Hundred
  Days**. ·RNW ¶12, ·NW ¶6
- A **seventh and final coalition** — Great Britain, Russia, Prussia, Austria — opposed him. **The campaign
  was brief.** ·RNW ¶12
- **Waterloo** — Napoleon's final defeat, by **Wellington's Dutch and English troops** and **Blücher's
  Prussians**. ·RNW ¶12, ·NW ¶6
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·RNW ¶12 — the source dates Waterloo "**June 16-18, 1815**"; the other source says only "June
  > 1815" ·NW ¶6. **The Battle of Waterloo was fought on 18 June 1815.** The 16th is **Ligny and Quatre
  > Bras**, two days earlier and at different places. The date range describes the campaign, not the battle.
  > Substantive, and exactly the kind of date a question turns on; **V5**.
- **The reason he lost, in the source's own analysis:** the same inability that had cost him Russia — he could
  not **surprise, and could not prevent the joining up of two armies invading along separate lines**. ·RNW ¶12
- **Napoleon abdicated on 22 June**; the **Bourbon monarchy was restored in Louis XVIII** shortly after. He
  was **exiled to Saint Helena** in the South Atlantic, where he **eventually died**. ·RNW ¶12, ·NW ¶6
- [added] Waterloo is not a departure from the pattern but its final case. Wellington and Blücher did to
  Napoleon what Wellington had done in Portugal and Barclay de Tolly in Russia: refuse the decisive battle on
  his terms, then combine. The examinable point is that his method was **understood and answered**, not that
  he was outnumbered — though by 1813 he was that too.

## 2.6 The Congress of Vienna and the legacy ·NW ¶7-14

### The settlement ·NW ¶7, ¶14

- The **Congress of Vienna (1814-1815)** reorganised the Europe left by roughly two decades of war. ·NW ¶7
- **The negotiators**, one per major power — learn these five as a set: ·NW ¶7

| Power | Negotiator |
|---|---|
| Austria | **Metternich** |
| Britain | **Castlereagh** |
| Russia | **Alexander I** |
| Prussia | **Hardenberg** |
| France | **Talleyrand** |

- The negotiations **created a stable Europe in which no one power could dominate the others** as Napoleon's
  France had. ·NW ¶7
- The resulting agreement was **one of the most important and complicated treaties in human history**; the
  order it designed was **balanced enough that future rising powers could be stopped by coalitions of other
  powers**. ·NW ¶14
- **But it also protected conservative regimes.** Napoleon had spread liberal change as he spread his empire;
  the kings and aristocrats at Vienna found a way to **prolong the life of the old conservative regimes**.
  ·NW ¶14
- The Congress therefore **set the stage for the coming battle between liberalism and conservatism** in the
  period from **1815 to the revolutionary year 1848**. ·NW ¶14
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·NW ¶7 — the source claims that not until **1914** would "another Europe-wide military conflict
  > break out". The **Crimean War (1853-56)** set Britain, France, Sardinia and the Ottoman Empire against
  > Russia, and the wars of Italian and German unification followed. The defensible claim is that no
  > *general* war involving all the great powers recurred until 1914 — as printed, it overstates. Substantive
  > enough to mislead; **V7**.

### What the wars changed ·NW ¶8-13

The second source is largely interpretive here. Treat these as **arguments to deploy**, not facts to recite.

**1. The Revolution became exportable.** ·NW ¶8, ¶10

- Europe's rulers had reason to worry: social levelling in France had destroyed **aristocratic privilege** and
  **executed a king**. If the reforms spread, conservative regimes would suffer. ·NW ¶8
- Reform made France **more efficient, powerful and patriotic** — opening leadership to all men **on the basis
  of talent** meant more of society had a stake in the country's future, and **the power of the masses was
  tapped as never before**. ·NW ¶8
- Occupied countries had versions of the **Napoleonic Code imposed on them**, forming the **legal basis for
  much of Continental European law today**. ·NW ¶10

**2. It spread to his enemies as well.** ·NW ¶10

- Reformers such as **Baron Stein and Hardenberg** realised that to compete with France they had to **build a
  Prussian state that resembled France**.
- So Napoleon **spread the ideas of the Revolution beyond the boundaries of his own empire**.
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·NW ¶10 — printed "spread to his opponents **to**"; read **too**. Cosmetic; **C25**.

**3. It mobilised nationalism — against him.** ·NW ¶11

- Reacting to French overlords, **previously disunited linguistic-ethnic groups found reason to organize**.
- The clearest case is **Germany**, which also **reacted intellectually, championing Romanticism** against the
  French Enlightenment rationalism Napoleon was spreading.
- The wars **fuelled liberal and conservative opponents alike**: Spanish guerrillas fought to restore a
  Bourbon; Germans complained they wanted more self-rule.

**4. Britain won.** ·NW ¶13

- Britain emerged in 1815 as a **commercial powerhouse** with the **world's pre-eminent navy** and a **large
  colonial network**.
- **British industry might have provoked working-class rebellion** but for the national unity that having an
  enemy like Napoleon provided. Blaming working-class hardship on Napoleon's warmongering, Britain **got
  through a critical and dangerous phase of its young Industrial Revolution**.
- [added] This is the most useful claim in either source for linking the two halves of the syllabus: it makes
  the Napoleonic Wars part of the explanation of why industrialization did *not* produce revolution in
  Britain. `01` opens by pairing the Revolution with industrialization ·FR p1 — this is where the pairing
  pays off.

**5. Its reach was global.** ·NW ¶9

- Napoleon **came closer than anyone else in modern history to conquering Europe**, and the conflict can be
  thought of as **an early kind of "world war"**.
- It **echoed in the New World**, influencing the **War of 1812** and **Toussaint l'Ouverture's regime in
  Haiti**.

**6. The moral verdict is deliberately unsettled.** ·NW ¶12

- The source argues that **right and wrong are hard to distinguish** in the period: Napoleon was a dictator
  **but not a particularly evil one**, and encouraged much we now consider positive.
- The wars were **instigated by France**, but **each nation fought to protect and expand its own national
  interest**, through **constantly shifting alliances** — Prussia sometimes fighting France and sometimes
  neutral; Austria under Metternich seeking better relations late on; Russia opposing, then siding with, then
  turning against Napoleon.
- **The only constant across the fifteen years was Anglo-French enmity.**
- The conclusion: **not a war between irreconcilable values but one driven on all sides by greed** — a period
  typified by **"Realism" in diplomacy and war**, every side simply seeking whatever advantage it could get.
- [added] Handle this paragraph carefully in an essay. "Not a particularly evil dictator" and "greed on all
  sides" are the **source's judgements**, not established fact, and an examiner will expect you to mark them
  as interpretation. They are also in tension with the same document's claim that Napoleon spread liberal
  reform wherever he went — if the wars were driven purely by greed, the reforms need another explanation.
  Pointing out that tension is a strong closing move.
