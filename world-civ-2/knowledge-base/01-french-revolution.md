# 01 — The French Revolution

The handout's argument in one line: the Revolution was not one event but a **collision of four separate
revolts** — an aristocratic revolt against the crown, a bourgeois revolt against the aristocracy, an urban
popular revolt, and a peasant revolt — which happened to coincide between 1787 and 1789, and which then
pulled apart again once the old regime had fallen.

Hold onto that. Nearly every question the handout sets can be answered by showing which revolt is doing the
work at a given moment, and where two of them stop pulling in the same direction.

**[added] Scope warning.** This handout ends at the declaration of war in April 1792. It names the Terror in
its very last line but never treats it, and it says nothing about Thermidor or the Directory. Anything you
need on 1793-1799 is **not in your notes** — `02` picks the story up again in 1799 with the Consulate.

## 1.1 Framing and interpretations ·FR p1

- Alongside industrialization, the French Revolution was a **major catalyst of the transformation of
  nineteenth-century Europe**. ·FR p1
- [hist] Ranked as an event in European life as comparable only to the **Protestant Reformation of the
  sixteenth century**. ·FR p1
- Its repercussions were felt far beyond France's frontiers, giving rise to **liberalism, socialism,
  democracy, fraternity and nationalism** — ideas that then spread worldwide. ·FR p1

### The four interpretations ·FR p1

The handout treats the Revolution as a subject of controversy and sets out four readings. Learn these as a
set: an essay that opens by naming them and then argues for one is doing exactly what the handout wants.

| # | Reading | Prime mover | Associated with |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The outcome of the machinations of irresponsible intellectuals against the time-honoured institutions of the old regime | ideas, destructively applied | **Edmund Burke** |
| 2 | The revolt of respectable middle-class property owners against the injustices of the old regime | the bourgeoisie, defensively | *(unattributed in the handout)* |
| 3 | A popular and spontaneous upheaval of the entire French nation, aiming to end the tyranny of mass poverty and build national sovereignty and popular democracy | the nation as a whole | **Jules Michelet** |
| 4 | Class struggle between a landed nobility defending the old order and a bourgeoisie seeking to abolish feudal restriction and open the way to modern capitalism | economic class conflict | **Marxist historians** |

- [added] Note the shape of the disagreement: readings 1 and 4 both make the Revolution *caused* — by ideas
  or by class — while readings 2 and 3 make it *reactive*, a response to injustice. That axis is the easiest
  way to structure a comparison question.
- [added] Burke is writing as a hostile contemporary (1790), Michelet as a nineteenth-century French
  republican, the Marxists as a twentieth-century school. Their dates are part of the answer: each
  interpretation belongs to its own moment.

## 1.2 Causes of the Revolution ·FR p1-p5

Five causes, in the handout's own order. Each is argued **with its own limits attached** — the handout never
lets a single cause carry the whole explanation, and neither should you.

### 1.2.1 The role of the intellectuals ·FR p1

- **Voltaire, Montesquieu and Rousseau** were extremely critical of the institutions of the old regime. They
  attacked the privileges of the church and nobility, advocated political reforms, and called for the
  abolition of feudalism. ·FR p1
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p1 — the handout prints "political eforms" (reforms), "the ideas of enlightment"
  > (Enlightenment), "the evens of 1789" (events) and "Rosseau" (Rousseau). Cosmetic; see C1-C4 in the log.
- **But the intellectuals can only be held responsible to a limited degree**, for three reasons: ·FR p1
  1. They envisaged **reform rather than revolution**.
  2. There was **no organized revolutionary movement** to translate Enlightenment ideas into a political
     programme.
  3. The **vast majority of those who acted in 1789 were illiterate** and had never heard of Montesquieu,
     Voltaire or Rousseau.
- The conclusion the handout draws: the intellectual ideas nonetheless supplied the **ideological inspiration
  and justification** for overthrowing the old regime. ·FR p1
- [added] The distinction to reproduce in an essay is between a **cause** and a **vocabulary**. The
  philosophes did not make the crowd act; they gave the act its language, and gave the men who wrote the
  Declaration of Rights their categories.

### 1.2.2 The failure of Louis XV and Louis XVI ·FR p2

- [def] Before 1789 the **absolute monarchy** was the centre of political power and decision-making in
  France. ·FR p2
- Both **Louis XV (reigned 1715-1774)** and **Louis XVI (reigned 1774-1793)** were weak and indecisive men,
  incapable of the fundamental political reform the old regime's structural problems required. ·FR p2
- **Louis XV** was more interested in food and women, and left decision-making and administration to
  courtiers who excelled only in flattery and intrigue. ·FR p2
- **Louis XVI** was incompetent and negligent, kept disastrous advisers, and loved hunting and drinking.
  ·FR p2
- The result: a **breakdown of royal authority**, creating the political vacuum into which the Revolution
  came. ·FR p2
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p2 — "weak of indecisive men" (and), "negligible" (negligent), "Loius XVI" (Louis).
  > Cosmetic; C5-C7.
- [added] The dates given are **reign dates**, not lifespans. Louis XVI's reign ends in 1793 because he was
  executed, which the handout does not say — it stops before the trial.

### 1.2.3 The financial crisis and the aristocratic revolt ·FR p2

- Through the eighteenth century France pursued a **costly foreign policy**, fighting and losing the **War of
  the Austrian Succession (1740-48)** and the **Seven Years War (1756-63)**. ·FR p2
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p2 — the handout calls this foreign policy "costly but **successful**" in the same sentence
  > that says France fought and lost both wars. The two halves contradict each other, and the Austrian
  > Succession is not a French defeat of the same kind as the Seven Years War: it ended at Aix-la-Chapelle
  > (1748) with conquests restored on both sides — expensive and fruitless rather than lost. Substantive;
  > **V3**.
- The consequence was a **huge national debt**, which by **1774** had become an intolerable burden on the
  economy. ·FR p2
- The debt was compounded by an **inequitable system of taxation**, under which the peasants and the
  bourgeoisie were the most heavily taxed sections of the population. ·FR p2
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p2 — the handout prints "the **persons** and bourgeoisie"; read **peasants**. The point of
  > the sentence is that the two *unprivileged* orders carried the tax burden. Cosmetic but meaning-obscuring;
  > **C8**.
- **Turgot**, Louis XVI's finance minister, proposed fiscal measures to avert the crisis, **including the
  removal of aristocratic tax exemptions**. The privileged nobility and the church opposed them. Lacking
  boldness, Louis XVI yielded to aristocratic pressure and **dismissed his reformist minister**. ·FR p2
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p2 — printed "Thurgot" (Turgot) and "aristocratic **task** exemptions" (tax). Cosmetic;
  > C9-C10.
- **Necker**, the new finance minister, resorted to **more public borrowing** to appease the aristocracy.
  ·FR p2
- The crisis deepened with the **ill-advised French intervention in the American War of Independence
  (1776-1783)**, which forced still more borrowing. ·FR p2
- Further proposals were **blocked by the church and the nobility**, paralysing government. ·FR p2
- [added] This is the section that earns the name **aristocratic revolt**: the first body to defeat the crown
  was not the Third Estate but the privileged orders, defending their exemptions. The Revolution begins as a
  revolt of the privileged, and only then passes to the bourgeoisie.

### 1.2.4 Bourgeoisie versus aristocracy ·FR p2-p3

**The setting** ·FR p2

- By **1788** royal power had almost collapsed, leaving a political vacuum. As a last resort Louis XVI's
  government decided to **convene the Estates-General**, which had not met since **1614**. ·FR p2
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p2, p4 — the handout twice prints "**convince** the Estates-General"; read **convene**.
  > Cosmetic; C11.
- The decision was an **abdication of royal power and an invitation to revolution**. ·FR p2
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p2 — printed "an **initiation** to revolution"; read **invitation**. Cosmetic; C12.
- It also opened a **historic confrontation between the bourgeoisie and the aristocracy**. ·FR p2
- [def] The **Estates-General** comprised three orders: the **Clergy** (First Estate), the **Aristocracy**
  (Second Estate) and the **Third Estate**, the commoners. ·FR p2
- The bourgeoisie were frustrated by the **lack of social mobility** that aristocratic exclusiveness imposed,
  and by 1789 French society was **polarized between privileged and non-privileged** classes. They therefore
  saw the Estates-General as their **opportunity to assert their own interests**. ·FR p3

**The procedural fight — this is where the Revolution actually starts** ·FR p3

| Question | Third Estate wanted | Clergy and nobility wanted | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Representation | more seats, as the majority of the nation | parity between orders | **600 seats to the Third Estate, 300 each to the other two** |
| One chamber or three? | one assembly, sitting together | separate orders | unresolved — deadlock |
| How to vote? | **by head**, simple majority | **by order**, which would outvote the Third Estate 2-1 | unresolved — deadlock |

- From **May to June 1789** the Estates-General was bogged down in this procedural disagreement. Instead of
  resolving the political crisis, the Third Estate and the aristocracy were locked in a bitter struggle for
  power. ·FR p3
- [added] The vote-by-head question looks technical and is not: doubling the Third Estate's seats is
  worthless if voting is by order, so the two concessions only mean something together. That is why the
  deadlock could not be split.

**The bourgeois breakthrough** ·FR p3

- **17 June 1789** — the Third Estate seized the initiative by constituting itself the **National Assembly**
  (renamed the **Constituent Assembly** in July) and invited the other two orders to join. ·FR p3
- **Two days later** the majority of the clergy voted to join. ·FR p3
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p3 — the handout prints "**This day later**", which is unreadable. The clergy voted to join
  > on **19 June 1789**, two days after the 17th. Substantive, because the interval is the fact; **V4**.
- The **nobility refused** and instead appealed to the king to restore legality. ·FR p3
- **23 June** — Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General, promised to make France a **constitutional
  monarchy**, but ordered the three orders to **meet separately** and ordered the **National Assembly
  dissolved**. ·FR p3
- The deputies **rejected the order** and resolved to sit until a new constitution was drawn up. ·FR p3
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p3 — the handout calls this "the famous Tennis court **resolution**" and places it **after**
  > the royal session of 23 June. Two corrections. The name is the **Tennis Court Oath** (Serment du Jeu de
  > Paume), and it was sworn on **20 June 1789** — *before* the royal session, not in answer to it. The
  > defiance on 23 June is a separate moment. Substantive sequencing error; **V2**.
- Faced with the bourgeois rebellion, **Louis XVI capitulated again** and ordered the nobility to join the
  National Assembly — the **political triumph of the bourgeoisie**, and the moment properly called the
  bourgeois revolution. ·FR p3
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p3 — printed "in what had been **caused** the bourgeois revolution"; read **called**.
  > Cosmetic; C13.

### 1.2.5 The economic crisis and the popular revolt ·FR p4-p5

**The depression** ·FR p4

- The collapse of the old regime **between 1787 and 1789** coincided with a major economic depression. ·FR p4
- A succession of **bad harvests** produced food shortages and soaring prices: the **price of bread doubled**,
  and **unemployment in Paris rose by 50%**. ·FR p4
- Adverse conditions sharpened peasant resentment of **high taxation and feudal obligations**. ·FR p4
- The decision to convene the Estates-General **raised popular expectations**, and compiling the lists of
  grievances **sharpened awareness of the injustices** of the old regime. ·FR p4
- In the spring of 1789 there were **spontaneous urban and peasant revolts** across the country. ·FR p4

**The handout's own qualification — do not miss this** ·FR p4

- The importance of the economic crisis **should not be over-emphasized**: France had suffered economic
  depression before 1789 without revolution. ·FR p4
- What precipitated popular intervention was **not the economic crisis as such but the fear of
  counter-revolution**. Louis XVI, though he had capitulated, was still determined to stage a coup and
  restore royal authority; **royal troops were stationed around Paris and Versailles**, and rumours of foreign
  intervention were rampant. ·FR p4
- So the people armed themselves **to forestall counter-revolution and defend the National Assembly**. ·FR p4
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p4 — printed "in order **put** forestall"; read **to forestall**. Cosmetic; C14.
- [added] This is the single most quotable move in the handout. Hunger explains the crowd's *existence*; fear
  of the king's troops explains its *timing and its target*. An essay that says "the people rose because they
  were hungry" has failed the handout's own argument.

**14 July 1789 and after** ·FR p4-p5

- Parisian crowds **stormed the Bastille on 14 July 1789**, symbolizing the fall of royal despotism. It was
  followed by the establishment of **civilian militias and municipal governments** throughout France. ·FR p4
- Popular intervention marked the **climax of the first phase**, and from then on political initiative passed
  **from the National Assembly to the popular forces** of the Revolution. ·FR p4
- Meanwhile a **distinct and autonomous revolution** was under way in the countryside. Peasant agitation
  predated the Bastille, but the urban triumph **sparked a major peasant upheaval**. ·FR p4
- Noble houses were **attacked, burned and their property destroyed**; forests were invaded. Agrarian revolt
  intensified as **law and order broke down**. ·FR p5
- By early **August 1789** peasants were armed with **guns, scythes and sticks**, demanding their share of the
  victory — **which the bourgeoisie were reluctant to grant**. ·FR p5
- [def] The **"Great Fear"** — rural France gripped by fear of an **aristocratic conspiracy**, of **foreign
  invasion**, and, distinctly, of **the intentions of the bourgeoisie** themselves. ·FR p5
- The handout's conclusion: the French peasants were **both allies and potential enemies** of the bourgeois
  revolution. ·FR p5
- [added] That last clause is the hinge of the whole section, and it is what the four-revolutions framing at
  the top of this file is for. The alliance that destroyed the old regime had no agreed programme for what
  came next.

## 1.3 The progress of the Revolution ·FR p5

Where things stood once the old regime had fallen:

- **Louis XVI** had reluctantly assumed the role of a **constitutional monarch**. ·FR p5
- The **nobility** had been reduced to **political impotence**. ·FR p5
- **Real power lay with the Constituent Assembly** — but it was threatened from two directions at once: the
  **forces of counter-revolution** and the **unpredictable revolutionary crowd**. ·FR p5
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p5 — the handout dates the king's assumption of a constitutional role to "By 1789". In 1789
  > he was constrained in practice but not yet bound in law; the constitution making him a constitutional
  > monarch was completed in **1791**. Read the sentence as describing his *de facto* position. Cosmetic
  > imprecision; **C15**.
- [added] Those two threats are the engine of everything in 1.5. The Assembly is squeezed, and each squeeze
  pushes it further from the moderate settlement it had just built.

## 1.4 The moderate phase, 1789-1791 ·FR p5-p7

- Between 1789 and 1791 the Constituent Assembly implemented a series of reforms that transformed the French
  **political, economic, social and religious landscape**. ·FR p5
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p5 — printed "initiated and **implanted**"; read **implemented**. Cosmetic; C16.
- The reforms drew on the **liberal and democratic ideas of the French intellectuals** and on the **American
  Bill of Rights**, and were guided by the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, August
  1789**. ·FR p5

### The Declaration of Rights — the eight the handout lists ·FR p5-p6

1. **Equality of all citizens before the law**, and the right of all citizens to make public laws.
2. **Freedom of speech and the press.**
3. **Freedom of conscience.**
4. **Freedom from arbitrary arrest and imprisonment.**
5. **An equitable system of taxation.**
6. **The right of rebellion** against oppression and bad government.
7. **The right to hold public office on the basis of talent rather than birth.**
8. **The right to private property.**

**What it was silent on** ·FR p6 — and the handout makes a point of the silences:

- freedom of **economic enterprise and trade**
- **popular democracy**
- **freedom of association**
- **slavery and the slave trade**

- [added] The silences are examinable in their own right. Items 7 and 8 together define the settlement's
  class character: careers open to talent, property untouched. That combination is exactly what the radicals
  of 1.5.4 will attack.

### The constitution ·FR p6

- France became a **constitutional monarchy**, on the premise that **sovereignty rested in the nation** —
  that is, in the **National Parliament** of elected representatives.
- The king **retained considerable powers**:
  1. Power to **appoint ministers, ambassadors and military commanders** — though the cabinet was answerable
     to parliament.
  2. Power to **declare war or make peace**, subject to parliamentary consent.
  3. Power to **suspend or delay legislation** other than finance, for **up to four years**.
     > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p6 — printed "Power to declare war **r** make peace" (or) and "Power **or** suspend"
     > (to). Cosmetic; C17-C18.
- The constitution granted **independence to the judiciary**, and established a **high court and a court of
  appeal**. ·FR p6

### Administrative, social and religious reform ·FR p6

- The old regime's administrative structure was abolished and a **uniform administrative system** created
  nationwide.
- **Feudalism, aristocratic privileges and immunities, nobility titles and the old orders** were swept away.
- **Clerical rights and privileges** were abolished.
- **Freedom of worship** was granted to religious minorities, notably **Protestants and Jews**.
- **Roman Catholic Church privileges** were abolished, leading to a **bitter state-church conflict**.
- [added] That conflict is not a side-note: it is the reason the Church appears in 1.5.1 on the
  counter-revolutionary side. The religious settlement of 1790 is what turns a large part of the clergy
  against the Revolution.

### Economic and financial reform ·FR p6-p7

- Church and noble **lands were divided and redistributed to the peasants**.
- All **feudal restrictions on the economy** were swept away.
- **Free trade in the domestic market** was introduced.
- All **internal tolls and customs posts** were removed.
- **External tariffs were retained** to protect national industries.
- An **equitable system of taxation** was introduced; **aristocratic and clerical tax exemptions abolished**.
- Old taxes — **church tithes and the salt tax** — were abolished.
- **Government bonds** were introduced to raise revenue, and were **soon used as bank notes**. ·FR p7
- [added] The internal free market with a protective external tariff is a coherent programme, and it is the
  bourgeois revolution written as economic policy: barriers between Frenchmen removed, barriers against
  foreigners kept.

## 1.5 The radical and violent phase, 1792-1793 ·FR p7-p9

- By the end of **1791 the Revolution appeared to be over**. Within a year the constitutional monarchy had
  been overthrown, France had become a **republic**, and the Revolution had entered its most violent phase.
  ·FR p7

The handout gives **five reasons** for the radicalization. Learn them as a causal chain, not a list: (a) and
(b) create fear, (c) supplies grievance, (d) supplies the political vehicle, (e) detonates the whole thing.

### 1.5.1 (a) The counter-revolution ·FR p7

- From the onset, the **nobility and to a large extent the Roman Catholic Church** bitterly opposed what they
  saw as the **bourgeois usurpation of power**.
- **Thousands of nobles fled** to neighbouring European countries. From exile they **openly advocated
  counter-revolution** to restore the old regime and **solicited foreign military and diplomatic support**.
- They **set up military training facilities and recruited soldiers** to invade France and overthrow the
  revolutionary regime.
- Internally, opposition came **especially from the Roman Catholic Church**, which openly supported the
  counter-revolution.
- The effect: **insecurity and deepened social division**. Supporters of the Revolution began to demand the
  **violent repression of its enemies, real or imaginary**.
- [added] "Real or imaginary" is the handout's phrase and it is doing deliberate work — it is the germ of the
  Terror, in a document that never reaches the Terror.

### 1.5.2 (b) Royalist plots ·FR p7-p8

- The counter-revolutionary movement was **encouraged and reinforced by the plots of Louis XVI** and his
  royalist advisers, at home and abroad. His **contempt for the bourgeois rulers** of revolutionary France was
  hardly concealed. ·FR p7
- Between **1789 and 1791** he **covertly solicited the military and diplomatic support of Austria, Prussia,
  Sweden and Spain** against the Revolution. ·FR p7
- Thwarted at home, he **attempted to flee the country** to join and lead the counter-revolution from abroad.
  He was **arrested and taken to Paris as a prisoner**. ·FR p8
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p8 — printed "**Limited** at home"; the sense required is *thwarted* or *frustrated*.
  > Cosmetic; C19.
- He became **more unpopular than ever**. ·FR p8
- The plots gave rise to a radical revolutionary movement that culminated in the **overthrow of the
  constitutional monarchy and the proclamation of the First French Republic in 1792**. ·FR p8
- [added] The flight is the **Flight to Varennes, June 1791**, and it matters for a reason the handout leaves
  implicit: a king who runs has abdicated the argument that he consents to the constitution. Republicanism
  becomes respectable from that week onward.

### 1.5.3 (c) The deepening economic crisis ·FR p8

- Despite the reforms of 1789-91, the **economic crisis continued unabated**.
- The Revolution **halted economic activity** and led to the **flight of capital** from the country.
- The peasant revolution and the revolutionary land settlement **disrupted the economy and froze the
  development of agrarian capitalism**.
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p8 — printed "the revolutionary land settlement **of** disrupted"; stray word. Cosmetic;
  > C20.
- The worsening situation **intensified revolutionary passions** and increased the influence of **extremists
  inside and outside the National Assembly**.
- The extremists **blamed the enemies of the Revolution** for the paralysis of the economy, and from **1792 to
  1793** launched a **violent campaign against the Revolution's economic enemies**.
- [added] Note the mechanism, because it recurs in every revolution on the syllabus: economic failure is
  explained politically, as sabotage, and the explanation licenses violence.

### 1.5.4 (d) Political divisions in the revolutionary camp ·FR p8

- From 1789 the Revolution's supporters were **divided about its goals and limits**.
- **Moderates** were content with the **constitutional monarchy**.
- **Radicals** held the Revolution **incomplete**, and advocated **universal manhood suffrage** and the
  **redistribution of wealth** to bring about economic equality.
- On **foreign policy** the split was equally sharp:
  - some politicians advocated **war as a way of exporting the Revolution** and of diverting the French people
    from domestic problems;
  - others feared that war between revolutionary France and Europe would **destroy the essence of the
    Revolution and lead to military dictatorship**.
- This sharpening of political contradictions was **a factor in the triumph of revolutionary extremism**.
- [added] The second group were right, and the handout knows it: `02` opens with the military dictatorship
  they predicted. If you are asked whether the Revolution failed, this is where the answer starts.

### 1.5.5 (e) The outbreak of the revolutionary war, April 1792 ·FR p9

- Since 1789 **European monarchs were outraged by the violation of the monarchical principle** and
  sympathized with Louis XVI.
- More importantly, they **feared French revolutionary ideas spreading** to their own countries, so France's
  neighbours **supported the counter-revolution**.
- After the **abortive escape attempt of June 1791**, **Austria and Prussia threatened war if Louis and his
  wife were harmed**, and stepped up support for the French counter-revolutionaries.
- For its part, revolutionary France calculated that war would **end the plots of the counter-revolutionaries**
  and **provide an opportunity to export the Revolution** and liberate the peoples of Europe from absolute
  monarchy.
- **In April 1792 France declared war**, to forestall foreign intervention.
  > ⚠ VERIFY ·FR p9 — the handout says France declared war "against **Prussia and Austria**". France declared
  > war on **20 April 1792 against Austria alone** (formally, against the King of Hungary and Bohemia).
  > **Prussia entered the war afterwards**, as Austria's ally. Substantive — the sequencing is the point, and
  > it is the kind of detail an examiner marks; **V1**.
- **Internally, the immediate victim was the monarchy.** ·FR p9
- The war **precipitated violent nationalist and revolutionary passions and inaugurated the reign of terror**,
  which marked the climax of the violent phase. ·FR p9
- [added] The Austria-and-Prussia threat of 1791 that the handout describes is the **Declaration of Pillnitz
  (August 1791)**. `02` cites it by name ·RNW ¶4, so the two files are describing the same document from
  opposite sides — useful if a question asks you to weigh French and European responsibility.
