# Self-test quizzes — World Civilization II

Answers are at the **foot of the file**, not beside the questions. Work a whole set before scrolling.

Marked **[trap]** are questions where the source material itself is wrong — the answer is the corrected fact,
and the log entry is named so you can check it. There are seven of these across the file, one for each
substantive flag.

## Set E — Source criticism

These have no single right answer; they test whether you can tell your material apart.

**QE1.** Your two reference articles date the wars differently — 1792-1815 and 1799-1815. What does each dating
imply about Napoleon's relationship to the Revolution?

**QE2.** The two sources explain the Russian disaster differently. State both, and say which is the better
answer and why.

**QE3.** One source calls Napoleon "a dictator, but not a particularly evil one" and says the wars were driven
by greed on all sides — and also says he spread liberal reform wherever he went. Why is that a problem?

**QE4.** Your handout stops in April 1792. Name three things that happened next which it does not cover.

# Answers

**Set A.** QA1 (b) Edmund Burke ·FR p1. — QA2 the Marxist historians' reading, interpretation 4 ·FR p1. —
QA3 they envisaged reform not revolution; there was no organised movement to turn Enlightenment ideas into a
programme; most participants in 1789 were illiterate and had never heard of them ·FR p1. — QA4 (a) 1614
·FR p2. — QA5 the War of the Austrian Succession (1740-48) and the Seven Years War (1756-63) ·FR p2. —
QA6 **[V3]** the same sentence says France fought and lost both wars, so "successful" contradicts it; and the
Austrian Succession was not a defeat of the Seven Years War kind — it ended at Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) with
conquests restored. Read "costly and unsuccessful". — QA7 **Turgot** (printed "Thurgot", **C9**); he proposed
removing aristocratic tax exemptions; the nobility and church opposed, and Louis XVI dismissed him ·FR p2. —
QA8 **Necker**, who borrowed more publicly to appease the aristocracy ·FR p2. — QA9 600 to the Third Estate,
300 each to clergy and nobility ·FR p3. — QA10 because if voting was **by order** rather than by head, the
other two orders would still outvote it 2-1; the two concessions only work together ·FR p3. —
QA11 **[V2]** **20 June 1789** — and the handout places it *after* the royal session of 23 June, reversing the
order, and calls it a "resolution" rather than an Oath. — QA12 **[V4]** **19 June 1789**, two days later; the
handout prints the unreadable "This day later". — QA13 unemployment up **50%**, bread **doubled** ·FR p4. —
QA14 **fear of counter-revolution** — royal troops around Paris and Versailles, and rumours of foreign
intervention ·FR p4. — QA15 aristocratic conspiracy, foreign invasion, and **the intentions of the
bourgeoisie** ·FR p5.

**Set B.** QB1 August 1789 ·FR p5. — QB2 freedom of economic enterprise and trade; popular democracy; freedom
of association; slavery and the slave trade ·FR p6. — QB3 up to **four years**, excluding **finance** ·FR p6. —
QB4 Protestants and Jews ·FR p6. — QB5 divided and redistributed them to the peasants ·FR p6. — QB6 they were
soon used as **bank notes** ·FR p7 (these are the *assignats*, which the handout never names). —
QB7 counter-revolution; royalist plots; the deepening economic crisis; political divisions in the revolutionary
camp; the outbreak of war in April 1792 ·FR p7-9. — QB8 enemies **"real or imaginary"** ·FR p7. — QB9 Austria,
Prussia, Sweden, Spain ·FR p7. — QB10 universal manhood suffrage and the redistribution of wealth to produce
economic equality ·FR p8. — QB11 that war would destroy the essence of the Revolution and lead to **military
dictatorship** ·FR p8 — and yes: `02` opens with it. — QB12 **[V1]** on **Austria alone**, 20 April 1792; the
handout says "Prussia and Austria". Prussia entered afterwards as Austria's ally. — QB13 1792 ·FR p8.

**Set C.** QC1 60,000-70,000; 250,000; some 600,000 ·RNW ¶4. — QC2 the decree placing all Frenchmen at the
army's disposal, producing armies of unprecedented size — it is why no single defeat was decisive against
France ·RNW ¶4. — QC3 1791; Austria and Prussia's call to European rulers to help Louis XVI re-establish
himself ·RNW ¶4. — QC4 Dumouriez and Kellermann, **20 September 1792**, against a Prussian-Austrian force
·RNW ¶4. — QC5 Austria, Prussia, Spain, the United Provinces, Great Britain ·RNW ¶4. — QC6 left the coalition
and signed a separate peace, which **held until 1806** ·RNW ¶6. — QC7 **March 1796**, because the previous
commander was reluctant to move ·RNW ¶6. — QC8 ceded the Austrian Netherlands (Belgium and Luxembourg) — the
Republic's first territorial additions — and recognised the Cisalpine and Ligurian republics ·RNW ¶7. —
QC9 **[V6]** the **Parthenopean Republic was at Naples**; the Piedmontese client state was the **Subalpine
Republic**. — QC10 Nelson's destruction of the French squadron at the **Battle of the Nile, 1 August 1798**,
which left Napoleon without naval support ·RNW ¶8. — QC11 **9 November 1799** ·RNW ¶8. — QC12 Marengo 1800; Ulm
1805; Austerlitz 1805; Jena-Auerstädt 1806; Friedland 1807 ·RNW ¶8, ·NW ¶3. — QC13 halved Prussia at the Elbe
and took part of its Poland; forced Alexander I into alliance ·RNW ¶8, ·NW ¶3. — QC14 Portugal, Sweden,
Sardinia, Sicily ·RNW ¶8. — QC15 the 1806 decree making ships liable to seizure if they reached
French-controlled ports after calling at British ones; it created the **Continental System** ·RNW ¶9. —
QC16 British retaliation froze European trade; licences for French merchants looked like favouritism and cost
political support; Britain expanded colonial markets and grew richer; it provoked German nationalism and the
Spanish Peninsular War ·RNW ¶9, ·NW ¶4. — QC17 *Method*: move fast, strike by surprise, prevent his enemies
coordinating, beat them piecemeal. *Answer*: keep a threat alive, refuse battle until coordinated, rely on
supply lines, and let his overextend ·RNW ¶11. — QC18 **Wellington**, in the Peninsula, supplied **through
Portugal** ·RNW ¶11. — QC19 **Borodino, 7 September 1812**, indecisive — Napoleon could not win a decisive
victory and was forced to retreat ·RNW ¶11. — QC20 **[V5]** **18 June 1815**. Two days earlier, on 16 June,
were **Ligny and Quatre Bras** — different battles at different places. One source's "June 16-18" describes
the campaign.

**Set D.** QD1 Metternich (Austria), Castlereagh (Britain), Alexander I (Russia), Hardenberg (Prussia),
Talleyrand (France) ·NW ¶7. — QD2 a balance in which no single power could dominate, stoppable by coalitions —
and it protected **conservative regimes** against the liberal change Napoleon had spread ·NW ¶7, ¶14. —
QD3 **[V7]** the **Crimean War (1853-56)** was a Europe-wide conflict, as were the Italian and German
unification wars. The defensible version: no **general war involving all the great powers** recurred until
1914. — QD4 nationalism and Romanticism ·NW ¶4, ¶11. — QD5 **Baron Stein and Hardenberg**, because they
realised that to compete with France they had to build a state resembling it ·NW ¶10. — QD6 because having an
enemy like Napoleon supplied national unity, and working-class hardship could be blamed on his warmongering
·NW ¶13. — QD7 the **War of 1812** and **Toussaint l'Ouverture's regime in Haiti** ·NW ¶9. — QD8 the battle
between liberalism and conservatism, **1815 to 1848** ·NW ¶14.

**Set E.** QE1 **[D1]** 1792-1815 makes the wars one continuous conflict and Napoleon the Revolution's heir;
1799-1815 makes the Napoleonic Wars their own thing and Napoleon its replacement. — QE2 **[D2]** one credits
Russian strategy (parallel withdrawal, overextended French supply lines, Borodino settling nothing); the other
credits the winter. The first is better: it explains why the retreat happened at all, and the winter then
destroyed an army that was already beaten and already retreating. — QE3 because the two claims pull against
each other — a war of pure greed does not explain a conqueror who exports legal equality. The tension is worth
naming in an essay rather than quoting both sides as if they agreed. — QE4 among others: the Terror, the fall
of the monarchy in August 1792 and the execution of Louis XVI, Thermidor, and the Directory — none of which
appears in any source you hold.
