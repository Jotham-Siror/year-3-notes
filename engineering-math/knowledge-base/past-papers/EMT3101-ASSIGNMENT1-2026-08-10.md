## Q1 — Stirling's formula

`[q]`
> By using the substitution $u = x + u\sqrt{x}$ in the integral definition of $\Gamma(x+1)$, show
> that for large values of $n$
> $$n! = \sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-n}n^{\,n+\frac12}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{N}.$$

> ⚠ **Paper erratum (P1)** — the substitution as printed is **self-referential**: $u$ appears on
> both sides. The integration variable has been lost. It should read
> $$t = x + u\sqrt{x} \qquad\text{in}\qquad \Gamma(x+1) = \int_0^{\infty} t^{x}e^{-t}\,dt$$
> with $u$ the *new* variable. **Recognising this is the whole first step of the question** — if you
> take the printed line literally there is nothing to substitute into.

**Solution.**

Start from the definition (file `01` §2.1), written in the integration variable $t$:

$$\Gamma(x+1) = \int_0^{\infty} t^{x}e^{-t}\,dt$$

Substitute $t = x + u\sqrt{x}$, so $dt = \sqrt{x}\,du$, and the limits $t: 0 \to \infty$ become
$u: -\sqrt{x} \to \infty$.

Write the integrand as a single exponential — this is the step that makes the whole thing work:

$$t^{x}e^{-t} = e^{\,x\ln t\;-\;t}$$

Now expand the exponent. Since $t = x\left(1 + \dfrac{u}{\sqrt x}\right)$,

$$\ln t = \ln x + \ln\left(1 + \frac{u}{\sqrt x}\right)$$

and using the standard series $\ln(1+z) = z - \tfrac{z^{2}}{2} + \tfrac{z^{3}}{3} - \cdots$ with
$z = u/\sqrt x$:

$$x\ln t = x\ln x + x\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt x} - \frac{u^{2}}{2x} + \frac{u^{3}}{3x^{3/2}} - \cdots\right) = x\ln x + u\sqrt x - \frac{u^{2}}{2} + \frac{u^{3}}{3\sqrt x} - \cdots$$

Subtract $t = x + u\sqrt x$. **The $u\sqrt x$ terms cancel** — which is why this substitution and no
other is the right one:

$$x\ln t - t = x\ln x - x - \frac{u^{2}}{2} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\right)$$

Therefore

$$\Gamma(x+1) = \sqrt{x}\,e^{\,x\ln x - x}\int_{-\sqrt x}^{\infty} e^{-u^{2}/2}\left[1 + O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt x}\right)\right]du$$

For large $x$ the lower limit $\to -\infty$ and the correction terms vanish, leaving the Gaussian
integral

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du = \sqrt{2\pi}$$

Hence

$$\Gamma(x+1) \approx x^{x}e^{-x}\sqrt{x}\cdot\sqrt{2\pi}$$

and setting $x = n$, with $\Gamma(n+1) = n!$ (file `01` §2.4):

$$\boxed{\;n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-n}n^{\,n+\frac12}\;}$$

> ⚠ **Paper erratum (P3)** — the paper writes this with an **equals sign**. Stirling's formula is
> **asymptotic**, not exact: the ratio $n!\,/\,\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-n}n^{n+1/2}$ tends to 1 but never
> reaches it. The proper statement uses $\sim$. Write $\approx$ or $\sim$ in your answer and say
> why — it is the kind of thing that earns a mark rather than losing one.

**Numerical check** *(ours)* — the ratio $n!/\text{Stirling}$:

| $n$ | $n!$ | Stirling | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 120 | 118.019 | 1.0168 |
| 10 | 3 628 800 | 3 598 696 | 1.0084 |
| 20 | $2.4329\times10^{18}$ | $2.4228\times10^{18}$ | 1.0042 |
| 50 | $3.04141\times10^{64}$ | $3.03634\times10^{64}$ | 1.0017 |

The error halves as $n$ doubles — consistent with the $O(1/n)$ correction the derivation dropped.

## Q2 — the "sophomore's dream"

`[q]`
> By considering standard power series expansions and using Gamma functions, show
> $$\int_0^{1} x^{x}\,dx = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{r^{r}}\right]$$
> You may assume that integration and summation commute.

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — turn $x^{x}$ into a series.** Write it as an exponential and use the standard expansion
$e^{z} = \sum z^{k}/k!$ (file `04` §1.1) with $z = x\ln x$:

$$x^{x} = e^{\,x\ln x} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(x\ln x\right)^{k}}{k!} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}\left(\ln x\right)^{k}}{k!}$$

**Step 2 — integrate one term, using a Gamma function.** Consider

$$I_k = \int_0^{1} x^{k}\left(\ln x\right)^{k}dx$$

Substitute $x = e^{-s}$, so $dx = -e^{-s}ds$ and $\ln x = -s$; the limits $x: 0 \to 1$ become
$s: \infty \to 0$, and the minus sign flips them back:

$$I_k = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-ks}(-s)^{k}e^{-s}\,ds = (-1)^{k}\int_0^{\infty} s^{k}e^{-(k+1)s}\,ds$$

The remaining integral is a Gamma function after one more scaling, $\sigma = (k+1)s$:

$$\int_0^{\infty} s^{k}e^{-(k+1)s}ds = \frac{1}{(k+1)^{k+1}}\int_0^{\infty}\sigma^{k}e^{-\sigma}d\sigma = \frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{(k+1)^{k+1}} = \frac{k!}{(k+1)^{k+1}}$$

so

$$I_k = \frac{(-1)^{k}\,k!}{(k+1)^{k+1}}$$

**Step 3 — sum.** The $k!$ cancels the $1/k!$ from the exponential series — which is the point of
the whole construction:

$$\int_0^{1}x^{x}dx = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\cdot\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{(k+1)^{k+1}} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)^{k+1}}$$

Re-index with $r = k+1$ (so $k = r-1$, and $k = 0$ becomes $r = 1$):

$$\boxed{\;\int_0^{1}x^{x}\,dx = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\,r-1}}{r^{r}}\;}$$

**Numerical check** *(ours)* — both sides to 30 digits:

$$\int_0^{1}x^{x}dx = 0.783430510712134407059264386527 = \sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{r-1}}{r^{r}}$$

Identical to every digit computed.

## Q3 — a shifted Beta integral

`[q]`
> **(a)** Show clearly that
> $$\int_a^{b}(b-x)^{\,m-1}(x-a)^{\,n-1}dx = (b-a)^{\,m+n-1}B(m,n)$$
> **(b)** Hence find the exact value of
> $$\int_3^{5}\sqrt{\frac{5-x}{x-3}}\;dx$$

> ⚠ **Paper erratum (P2)** — **both parts are lettered "(a)"** on the paper. The second is (b); the
> word "Hence" makes the intended order unambiguous.

### (a)

**Solution.** The Beta function is defined on $[0,1]$ (file `01` §5.1), so map $[a,b]$ onto it.
Substitute

$$x = a + (b-a)t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad dx = (b-a)\,dt$$

with $t: 0 \to 1$ as $x: a \to b$. The two factors become

$$b - x = (b-a)(1-t) \qquad\qquad x - a = (b-a)t$$

Substituting all three:

$$\int_0^{1}(b-a)^{\,m-1}(1-t)^{\,m-1}\cdot(b-a)^{\,n-1}t^{\,n-1}\cdot(b-a)\,dt$$

Collect the powers of $(b-a)$ — there are $(m-1) + (n-1) + 1 = m+n-1$ of them, and they come out of
the integral as a constant:

$$= (b-a)^{\,m+n-1}\int_0^{1}t^{\,n-1}(1-t)^{\,m-1}dt = (b-a)^{\,m+n-1}B(n,m)$$

and by symmetry, $B(n,m) = B(m,n)$ (file `01` §5.3):

$$\boxed{\;\int_a^{b}(b-x)^{\,m-1}(x-a)^{\,n-1}dx = (b-a)^{\,m+n-1}B(m,n)\;}$$

### (b)

**Solution.** First put the integrand into the shape of part (a) — the square root of a quotient is
two separate powers:

$$\sqrt{\frac{5-x}{x-3}} = (5-x)^{1/2}(x-3)^{-1/2}$$

Now read off the parameters against $(b-x)^{m-1}(x-a)^{n-1}$ with $a = 3$, $b = 5$:

$$m - 1 = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow m = \tfrac32 \qquad\qquad n - 1 = -\tfrac12 \Rightarrow n = \tfrac12$$

Check the exponent of $(b-a)$: $m+n-1 = \tfrac32 + \tfrac12 - 1 = 1$, and $b - a = 2$. So

$$\int_3^{5}\sqrt{\frac{5-x}{x-3}}\,dx = 2^{1}\,B\!\left(\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right) = 2\cdot\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac32\right)\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac12\right)}{\Gamma(2)}$$

With $\Gamma(3/2) = \tfrac12\sqrt\pi$, $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt\pi$ and $\Gamma(2) = 1! = 1$:

$$= 2\cdot\frac{\tfrac12\sqrt\pi\cdot\sqrt\pi}{1} = 2\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}$$

$$\boxed{\;\int_3^{5}\sqrt{\frac{5-x}{x-3}}\;dx = \pi\;}$$

**Note that $n = \tfrac12$ makes this an improper integral** — the integrand blows up at $x = 3$.
The Beta function handles it without comment because $n > 0$ is all the definition requires. Saying
so is worth a line in an answer.

**Numerical check** *(ours)*: direct integration gives $3.14159265358979321$ against
$\pi = 3.14159265358979324$ — agreement to 17 significant figures (the small gap is the quadrature's
handling of the endpoint singularity, not a discrepancy).

## Q4 — Maclaurin, then term-by-term integration

`[q]`
> Use Maclaurin's theorem to expand $\cos 2\theta$ and hence evaluate, correct to 2 decimal places,
> $$\int_0^{1}\frac{\cos 2\theta}{\theta^{1/3}}\,d\theta$$

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — expand $\cos2\theta$.** By Maclaurin's theorem (file `04` §1.2) with
$f(\theta) = \cos2\theta$:

| | at $\theta = 0$ |
|---|---|
| $f = \cos2\theta$ | $1$ |
| $f' = -2\sin2\theta$ | $0$ |
| $f'' = -4\cos2\theta$ | $-4$ |
| $f''' = 8\sin2\theta$ | $0$ |
| $f^{(\mathrm{iv})} = 16\cos2\theta$ | $16$ |

$$\cos2\theta = 1 - \frac{4\theta^{2}}{2!} + \frac{16\theta^{4}}{4!} - \cdots = 1 - 2\theta^{2} + \frac{2}{3}\theta^{4} - \frac{4}{45}\theta^{6} + \cdots$$

or in general $\displaystyle\cos2\theta = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2\theta)^{2k}}{(2k)!}$.

**Step 2 — divide by $\theta^{1/3}$ and integrate term by term** (file `04` §3):

$$\frac{\cos2\theta}{\theta^{1/3}} = \theta^{-1/3} - 2\theta^{5/3} + \tfrac23\theta^{11/3} - \tfrac{4}{45}\theta^{17/3} + \cdots$$

$$\int_0^{1}\theta^{\,2k-1/3}d\theta = \frac{1}{2k + \tfrac23}$$

so the whole integral is

$$\int_0^{1}\frac{\cos2\theta}{\theta^{1/3}}d\theta = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\,4^{k}}{(2k)!\left(2k + \tfrac23\right)}$$

**Step 3 — evaluate.** The terms fall away quickly:

| $k$ | term | running total |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $+1.500000$ | 1.500000 |
| 1 | $-0.750000$ | 0.750000 |
| 2 | $+0.142857$ | 0.892857 |
| 3 | $-0.013333$ | 0.879524 |
| 4 | $+0.000732$ | 0.880256 |
| 5 | $-0.0000265$ | 0.880230 |

$$\boxed{\;\int_0^{1}\frac{\cos2\theta}{\theta^{1/3}}\,d\theta = 0.88 \ \text{(2 d.p.)}\;}$$

**Note the integrand is singular at $\theta = 0$** — $\theta^{-1/3} \to \infty$ — yet the integral
converges, because $-1/3 > -1$. The series method handles this without any special treatment, which
is exactly why the question is set this way rather than with a numerical rule.

**Numerical check** *(ours)*: direct quadrature gives $0.8802306176902479$; the series above sums to
$0.8802306176902479$. Identical to 16 significant figures.

## Q5 — a double integral over a triangle

`[q]`
> Determine the exact value of $I$ by transforming it into an expression involving Beta functions.
> $$I = \int_0^{1}\int_0^{1-x} xy\left(1 - x - y\right)^{1/2}dy\,dx$$

**Solution.** Take the inner integral first, holding $x$ fixed.

**Step 1 — the inner integral.** Over $y \in [0, 1-x]$, substitute

$$y = (1-x)\,t \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad dy = (1-x)\,dt, \qquad t: 0 \to 1$$

Then $1 - x - y = (1-x)(1-t)$, and

$$\int_0^{1-x} y\left(1-x-y\right)^{1/2}dy = \int_0^{1}(1-x)t\cdot(1-x)^{1/2}(1-t)^{1/2}\cdot(1-x)\,dt$$

$$= (1-x)^{5/2}\int_0^{1}t^{\,2-1}(1-t)^{\,3/2-1}dt = (1-x)^{5/2}\,B\!\left(2,\tfrac32\right)$$

Evaluate that Beta:

$$B\!\left(2,\tfrac32\right) = \frac{\Gamma(2)\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac32\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac72\right)} = \frac{1\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi}{\tfrac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi} = \frac{4}{15}$$

**Step 2 — the outer integral.** What remains is another Beta:

$$I = \frac{4}{15}\int_0^{1}x\,(1-x)^{5/2}dx = \frac{4}{15}\int_0^{1}x^{\,2-1}(1-x)^{\,7/2-1}dx = \frac{4}{15}\,B\!\left(2,\tfrac72\right)$$

$$B\!\left(2,\tfrac72\right) = \frac{\Gamma(2)\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac72\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{11}{2}\right)} = \frac{\tfrac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi}{\tfrac{945}{32}\sqrt\pi} = \frac{15}{8}\cdot\frac{32}{945} = \frac{4}{63}$$

$$I = \frac{4}{15}\cdot\frac{4}{63}$$

$$\boxed{\;I = \frac{16}{945} \approx 0.016931\;}$$

**Cross-check by the Dirichlet form** *(ours — not required, but it is one line).* For the standard
simplex,

$$\iint_{x,y\ge0,\ x+y\le1} x^{\,p-1}y^{\,q-1}(1-x-y)^{\,r-1}dy\,dx = \frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)\Gamma(r)}{\Gamma(p+q+r)}$$

Here $p = 2$, $q = 2$, $r = \tfrac32$, so

$$I = \frac{\Gamma(2)\Gamma(2)\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac32\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{11}{2}\right)} = \frac{\tfrac12\sqrt\pi}{\tfrac{945}{32}\sqrt\pi} = \frac{16}{945} \ ✓$$

**Numerical check** *(ours)*: nested numerical integration gives $0.01693121693121693$ against
$16/945 = 0.01693121693121693$ — identical to 17 significant figures.
