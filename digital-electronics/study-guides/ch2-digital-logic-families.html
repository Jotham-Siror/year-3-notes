1 · The spine of the chapter

Chapter 2 answers one question: what makes one logic family different from another? The answer is seven parameters, then six circuit technologies compared against them.

The six technologies are presented as a build — each one fixes the previous one's flaw. If you remember only this chain, you can reconstruct most of the chapter:

TechnologyWhat it addsIts flaw
Diode logicgates from diodes and a resistoronly AND and OR; loses 0.7 V per stage, no gain to restore it
RTLa transistor — now you get NORpoor speed, poor noise margin, high power
DTLdiode-AND feeding an inverting transistor, so the level is restoredslower than TTL, more components
TTLreplaces input diodes with a multi-emitter transistor; totem-pole outputhigh power, fixed 5 V supply
Open-collector / tri-stateoutput-stage variants that allow wired logic and shared busesneeds an external pull-up / needs enable control
CMOScomplementary MOSFETs — no DC path to ground in either stateclassically slower; sensitive to static
The whole chapter contains five equations. Everything else is circuits, tables and definitions. If the equations feel thin, that is because they are — the marks here are in drawing and explaining circuits.

3 · Logic levels — where the four symbols come from

A real gate does not switch at one exact voltage. Its output-versus-input curve is a smooth S, not a vertical step, and near the middle the output is neither a clean 1 nor a clean 0.

So lines are drawn on the voltage axis. And you need two sets of them, because:

  • a gate's output is a promise about what it will produce;
  • a gate's input is a promise about what it will accept.

Two promises × two logic states = four levels.

Decoding the symbols

Don't memorise them — read them. Every symbol is V followed by two letters: first letter I for input, O for output; second letter H for high, L for low.

SymbolThe promise it makesWhich extreme
VOH“When I output a 1, it will be at least this high.”a minimum
VOL“When I output a 0, it will be no more than this.”a maximum
VIH“Give me at least this and I'll read it as a 1.”a minimum
VIL“Anything up to this I'll still read as a 0.”a maximum

Anything landing between VIL and VIH is the undefined band. Behaviour there is unpredictable and no design may rely on it.

The standard levels (slide 9)

These are supplied in a question — you are not expected to recall all twelve. Learn the TTL column and the fact that 3.3 V CMOS is identical to it (which is why HCT and ACT parts are called “TTL-compatible”).

VIL (max)0.8 V1.5 V0.8 V
VOH (min)2.4 V4.4 V2.4 V

4 · Noise margins — worked

A signal travels down a wire and picks up noise. How much can it pick up before the receiving gate misreads it? Read the answer straight off this map.

HIGH state. The driver puts out at least 2.4 V; the receiver only needs 2.0 V. 0.4 V of slack — noise can drag the signal down that far before the 1 is lost.

LOW state. The driver puts out at most 0.4 V; the receiver tolerates up to 0.8 V. Again 0.4 V of slack.

NMH = VOH − VIH
NML = VIL − VOL
One rule covers both, so you never have to recall the order:
noise margin = what is guaranteed − what is required.
A noise margin is always positive. A negative answer means you subtracted the wrong way — or the two families genuinely cannot drive each other, which is itself a valid answer in an interfacing question.

Worked — Example 1, slide 13

TTL

NMH = 2.4 − 2.0
NMH = 0.4 V
NML = 0.8 − 0.4
NML = 0.4 V

5 V CMOS

NMH = 4.4 − 3.5
NMH = 0.9 V
NML = 1.5 − 0.33
NML = 1.17 V

What it tells you. CMOS tolerates roughly three times the noise TTL does. These four numbers are the evidence behind the “CMOS noise immunity high, TTL low” row of the comparison table. If asked why CMOS has better noise immunity, quote them rather than describing.

Naming clash — C02-1 Slide 12 calls these NML and NMH; slide 13 works the same example calling them VNL and VNH. Same two quantities, both names in your deck.

5 · Propagation delay

Propagation delay is measured between the 50% point of the input and the 50% point of the output. Rise and fall times use the 10% and 90% points instead — different reference levels, and mixing them up is a standard way to lose a mark.

V50% = ( VH + VL ) / 2
τP = ( τPHL + τPLH ) / 2
The subscript rule. HL and LH describe the direction the output moves, never the input. τPHL is the delay on the edge where the output falls from high to low.
Slide error — V02-3 Slide 10's two written glosses are swapped: it describes τPHL as “going from logical 0 to logical 1” and τPLH as the reverse. The subscripts and the waveform beside them are correct; the words are not. Read the subscripts, not the words.

6 · Power dissipation — worked

A TTL gate draws a different supply current depending on what its output is doing — output HIGH, the pull-up conducts; output LOW, the pull-down conducts through a different path. One gate, two supply currents, both quoted on the datasheet.

To collapse that into one power figure you must assume how the gate splits its time. The manufacturer's assumption is a 50% duty cycle — HIGH half the time, LOW half the time. State that assumption in your answer; it earns the mark.

ICC(avg) = ( ICCH + ICCL ) / 2
PD(avg) = VCC × ICC(avg)

Worked — Example 2, slide 17

A gate draws 2 mA with its output HIGH and 3.6 mA with its output LOW. Find the average power dissipation at VCC = 5 V on a 50% duty cycle.

ICC = ( 2 mA + 3.6 mA ) / 2
ICC = 2.8 mA
PD = 5 V × 2.8 mA
PD = 14 mW
Slide error — V02-4 · costs marks The slide asks the question in milliamps and then solves it in microamps, printing 2.8 µA and 14 µW. The digits are right; the prefix is wrong by a factor of 1000. The answer is 14 mW. If a marking scheme is built from the slide it will expect 14 µW — write 14 mW and note that the data given is in mA.

7 · Diode logic, and why it died

GateDiode orientationResistorWhy it works
ORanodes at the inputs, cathodes commoned at the outputR to ground any input HIGH forward-biases its diode and pulls the output up; all inputs LOW leaves every diode off and R holds the output at 0
ANDcathodes at the inputs, anodes commoned at the outputR to +5 V any input LOW forward-biases its diode and clamps the output down; only with every input HIGH do all diodes turn off and R pulls the output up

The level walk — the quantitative argument

A diode gate contains no transistor, so it has no gain. Every stage costs one forward drop — about 0.7 V in silicon — and nothing anywhere puts it back.

Vout = Vin − 0.7
Added — not in your deck The deck states only the first two steps (4.3 V and 3.6 V). Carrying the chain further is what makes the argument land, and it is worth writing out if you are asked to justify why diode logic is obsolete.

The three reasons the deck gives (slide 22)

  1. It permits only AND and OR — NAND and NOR arise only as special cases.
  2. It suffers a voltage drop from one stage to the next.
  3. It is usable for simple stand-alone logic but not inside integrated circuits.
The link to the next section. The fix is to add a transistor to restore the level — and that is exactly what DTL is. One sentence, and it connects two sections of the chapter.

8 · RTL — resistor-transistor logic

Operation. Either input HIGH drives base current through its resistor, saturates that transistor and pulls the output down to VCE(sat) — a logic 0. Only with both inputs LOW are both transistors off, and RC pulls the output up to a logic 1. That is NOR.

Identify the gates — the slide-24 exercise

CircuitWhat to look atAnswer
(i)one input into one common-emitter stageInverter, Q = A′
(ii)two inputs summed by resistors into one baseNOR, Q = (A + B)′
(iii)two emitter followers stacked in series — current reaches the output only if both conductAND, Q = A·B (the slide prints this one)
Slide error — C02-2 Slide 24 shows four circuits but only three distinct ones — the bottom-left and bottom-right schematics are identical down to the component labels. Answer three, not four.
Added — the mark on the base lead The solid mark on each base lead in circuits (i) and (ii) is a diode in series with the base, anode at the summing node. It is there because R1 returns the base to a negative rail; without it, inputs LOW would reverse-bias the emitter-base junction, whose reverse breakdown is only about 5–7 V. Circuit (iii) runs from +VCC alone, has no negative rail, and carries no diode — which corroborates the reading.

9 · DTL — diode-transistor logic

Operation.

  • Any input LOW — that diode pulls the common node down. The node cannot reach the voltage needed to drive the base, R3 holds the base at −VBB, the transistor is off, and RL pulls the output HIGH.
  • All inputs HIGH — every diode turns off, R1 pulls the node up, current flows into the base, the transistor saturates, and the output goes LOW.

That is NAND. The capacitor across R2 is a speed-up capacitor — it passes the leading edge of the base drive straight through, shortening turn-on time.

The slide-27 example — identify the function

The orientation decides the answer, and it is reversed from the NAND above. Here the diode anodes face the inputs and the cathodes are commoned. So a diode conducts only when its own input is driven HIGH — the common node is a diode OR, feeding an inverting stage. OR then NOT gives NOR.

10 · TTL — the family the chapter is built around

Introduced by Texas Instruments in 1964. The NAND gate is the family's basic building block. The switching model used throughout: a saturated transistor is a closed switch to ground, an OFF transistor is an open switch.

Input HIGH → output LOW

  • Q1's base-emitter junction is reverse-biased; its base-collector junction conducts instead and passes current into the phase splitter.
  • Three stacked forward drops fix the node voltages: 3 × 0.7 = 2.1 V at Q1's base, 1.4 V at the phase splitter's base, 0.7 V at the pull-down's base.
  • Phase splitter ON, pull-down ON, output at VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V.
  • Pull-up OFF: turning it on would need 0.2 + 0.7 + 0.7 = 1.6 V at its base, and the saturated phase splitter holds its collector far below that.

Input LOW → output HIGH

  • Q1's base-emitter junction conducts, base at 0.7 V, and it saturates — so no current reaches the phase splitter.
  • Phase splitter OFF, pull-down base at 0 V so pull-down OFF.
  • R2 pulls the pull-up's base up; it turns ON and sources the output HIGH.

The level-shift diode exists to guarantee the two output transistors are never on together — it adds a drop to the voltage the pull-up needs before it can conduct.

Slide error — C02-5 Slide 29(a) labels the phase-splitter collector “≅ 0.7 V”. With that transistor saturated the node sits at 0.7 + 0.2 = 0.9 V. The conclusion is unchanged — either figure is well below the 1.6 V needed — but quote 0.9 V.
The multi-emitter transistor is a fabrication trick, not a new device. Its equivalent is a diode network — a diode from base to each emitter, plus one from base to collector — which is exactly the diode-AND of section 7. That is why TTL's input stage behaves like DTL's.
Slide error — C02-4 The totem-pole numbering changes between slides. On slides 29 and 30 the pull-up is Q4 and the pull-down Q3; on slide 31 they are swapped. Identify the devices by position — upper is the pull-up, lower is the pull-down — never by number.

Slide 30 — identify the gate

The inverter drawn with a two-emitter input transistor, one emitter per input. Either emitter pulled LOW turns the input transistor on and starves the phase splitter, so the output goes HIGH; only with both inputs HIGH does the output go LOW.

A two-input TTL NAND with a totem-pole output

What has been deleted: the pull-up transistor, the level-shift diode and the 130 Ω collector resistor — the entire upper half of the totem pole. The output transistor's collector is brought out bare.

Consequence: the gate can pull its output LOW but cannot drive it HIGH at all. An external pull-up resistor must be fitted for the output to reach a valid 1. The symbol is a diamond-and-bar inside the gate body.

11 · CMOS

Complementary metal-oxide semiconductor — two types of transistor in the output circuit, one n-channel and one p-channel.

DeviceTurns ON when
n-channel enhancementgate is higher than source
p-channel enhancementgate is lower than source
  • Input LOW — p-channel ON, n-channel OFF, output pulled to VDD.
  • Input HIGH — p-channel OFF, n-channel ON, output pulled to ground.
The key sentence, and it is worth a mark on its own: neither steady state leaves a conducting path from VDD to ground, which is why CMOS static power is negligible.
One rule generates both circuits — learn this instead of memorising two schematics.
The n-channel network and the p-channel network are duals: whatever the pull-down does in series, the pull-up does in parallel, and vice versa.
Gaten-channel (pull-down)p-channel (pull-up)Why
NANDseriesparalleloutput pulled LOW only when both inputs are HIGH
NORparallelserieseither input HIGH pulls the output LOW

Why CMOS (slide 37)

  • very low static power — no DC path in either steady state
  • full rail-to-rail output swing
  • scales well, which is what makes large-scale integration possible

Two cautions from the same slide: never leave inputs floating (in TTL a floating input floats HIGH; in CMOS the result is genuinely undefined), and CMOS is susceptible to electrostatic damage.

Slide error — V02-5 Slide 36 draws both enhancement stages with the source grounded. That is right for the n-channel device and wrong for the p-channel one — with its source at 0 V it would need a negative gate voltage, which a 0–5 V logic system can never supply. The p-channel source belongs at VDD, which is exactly how it is drawn in the inverter two slides later. The deck contradicts itself here.

14 · Quick-recall lists

Classification of the families (slide 20)

BranchSub-branchFamilies
Unipolar (MOSFETs)—PMOS, NMOS, CMOS
Bipolar (BJTs)saturatedRTL, DTL, TTL, HTL, DCTL
unsaturatedSchottky TTL, ECL

Why the unsaturated branch is the fast one: keeping a transistor out of saturation removes the stored-charge delay of pulling it back out. That is why Schottky TTL sits there rather than beside standard TTL.

Fan-in and fan-out

  • Fan-in — number of inputs the gate provides. A three-input XOR has fan-in 3.
  • Fan-out — number of inputs one output can drive. Standard TTL is about 10.

Current sourcing and sinking

Driver outputCurrent directionSymbol
SourcingHIGHflows out of the driver into the load inputIIH, ~40 µA
SinkingLOWflows out of the load input into the driverIIL, ~1.6 mA
The exam-worthy consequence: in TTL the sink current per load is far larger than the source current, so the LOW state is what limits fan-out.

Unused inputs (slide 19)

Never leave them floating. Tie them to VCC through about 1.0 kΩ, or tie them to ground. Which one depends on the gate: an unused NAND input is tied HIGH, an unused NOR input is tied LOW — both leave the function of the remaining inputs untouched.

Homework the deck sets (slide 43)

ECL circuits and PMOS / NMOS / E²CMOS are named in the classification but taught nowhere in the deck — they are set as textbook reading. If an exam covers them, the deck is not your source.

Question 2 — truth table and gate identification (4 marks)

Develop the truth table for the circuit in figure 1 and determine its logic gate.

The exam answer

ABQ1Q2Q3Q4Output
00saturatedoffoffon1
01saturatedoffoffon1
10saturatedoffoffon1
11reverse activeononoff0
Z = ( A · B )′ — a two-input NAND gate

The explanation

Any input LOW. That emitter's base-emitter junction is forward biased, so Q1 saturates and its collector — the base of Q2 — is pulled down to about 0.2 V. That is far below the ≈ 1.4 V needed to turn on Q2 and Q3 in series, so both stay off. The pull-up's base sits near VCC through R3, it conducts, and the output goes HIGH.

Both inputs HIGH. Neither emitter can conduct, so current from R1 flows through Q1's forward-biased base-collector junction into Q2 — Q1 is in reverse active mode. Q2 turns on, its emitter current drives Q3 into saturation, and the output is pulled down to VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V. At the same time the pull-up turns off, so the two output transistors are never on together.

Only the all-HIGH row gives a LOW output. That is NAND.

The two diodes on the inputs take no part in the logic. They are clamps that conduct only if an input is driven below ground, limiting negative undershoot. Say so — it shows you read the circuit rather than recognised it.

Question 3 — draw a DTL NAND gate (4 marks)

Draw a Diode Transistor Logic for a NAND gate.

Two of the four marks are for the circuit and two for the explanation. Draw it and say how it works.

What to draw, as a checklist

  • inputs A and B, each through a diode with its cathode at the input, anodes joined at a common node P;
  • R1 from +VCC down to P;
  • two series level-shifting diodes from P to the base of an npn transistor;
  • R2 from that base to ground;
  • RC from +VCC to the collector, which is the output;
  • emitter to ground.

The operation, one line each

Any input LOW — that input diode conducts and holds P at about 0.7 V, which cannot supply the 2.1 V needed across the two series diodes and VBE. The transistor is off and the output is HIGH.

All inputs HIGH — both input diodes are reverse biased, P rises, current flows through the series diodes into the base, the transistor saturates and the output is LOW.

Z = ( A · B )′ — a NAND

Why the two series diodes are there — the marks-earning sentence

They set the turn-on threshold. Each adds one forward drop to the voltage P must reach before any base current flows:

VP(on) = 0.7 + 0.7 + 0.7 = 2.1 V
VP(input LOW) ≈ 0.7 V

So the LOW state sits 1.4 V clear of the turn-on threshold. Without the series diodes, P at 0.7 V would already be starting to turn the transistor on, and the gate would have essentially no noise margin.

Do not copy the wrong slide ·CH2 slide 26 is the DTL NAND — that is the one this question wants. ·CH2 slide 27 draws a DTL NOR: the diode orientation is reversed there (anodes at the inputs). Copying slide 27 answers a different question.
